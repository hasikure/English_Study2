English Text

At forty-six, my aunt decided to learn the piano, a plan most of her friends quietly assumed she would abandon within a month. She had never played an instrument in her life, and undoubtedly she was starting later than almost anyone in her local music shop's beginner class.

Private lessons were far too expensive on her salary, so she chose to substitute a private teacher for an interactive app that listened to her playing through the microphone and corrected her in real time. It cost a flat monthly fee, no matter how many hours she practised, which suited someone who had no idea, at the start, how much practice she would actually need.

What surprised her most was how much the app pushed her to cooperate with other beginners in an online group, sharing short recordings and comparing clumsy early attempts. At first this felt humiliating. Her own recordings sounded, to her ears, hopelessly amateurish next to some of the younger members. She was tempted to dwell on every mistake, replaying the worst moments long after everyone else had moved on.

A stranger in the group finally told her something that changed how she practised: stop listening for perfection, and start listening for small, deep improvements from one week to the next. She began comparing her own recordings only to her own recordings from a month earlier, never to anyone else's.

Two years later, she still cannot play anything difficult quickly, and she has made peace with that. What she has instead is a slow, steady, deep kind of competence that came from showing up, week after week, undoubtedly slower than a child would have managed, but real all the same. Late starts, it turns out, are rarely as hopeless as everyone quietly assumes.
