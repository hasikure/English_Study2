Script

Last Saturday, I visited a small recording studio near the train station. My friend Lena had invited me to watch a youth orchestra prepare music for a charity radio show. The room was full of wires, music stands, and paper cups of tea. In the center stood an elderly maestro named Mr. Kido. He had spent a lifetime composing short melodies for films, but he spoke in a calm, simple way.

Before rehearsal, he asked each student why they had come. One girl said she was there of her own will, not because her parents pushed her. The maestro smiled and said that was the best reason to study art. During the first song, a violin player missed her first note and looked embarrassed. Mr. Kido did not exalt only the strongest performers. Instead, he stopped the group and showed everyone how to breathe together and follow the piano.

By the end of the session, the music sounded warmer and more confident. On the whole, I learned something surprising that day. Great teachers are not famous because they demand perfection. They are remembered because they help ordinary people create something beautiful together.
