Script

At the beginning of the summer, I visited a small exhibition about a scientist I had never heard of before. She lived about two hundred years ago and became one of the early pioneers of microscopy, studying tiny living things that most people did not even believe existed.

What I liked most was that she was, in her own quiet way, a rebel. At the time, respectable science was done by wealthy men in universities. She had no such position, so she simply built her own instruments at home and embarked on years of careful observation. When she sent her drawings to a famous society, several members refused to believe a woman had made them.

The exhibition was honest about how slow her recognition was. For most of her life, she was ignored or laughed at. It was only decades later that people began to call her a genius, once her results had been confirmed again and again by others.

Walking home, I thought about how strange fame is. The word "genius" is usually given at the end of a long, lonely process, not at the start. She did not feel like a genius while she worked. She just kept looking, patiently, at things nobody else bothered to see.
