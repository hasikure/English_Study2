English Text

Few people outside of Millbrook have heard of Walter Hale, but almost everyone who grew up nearby can recollect at least one childhood afternoon lost inside his famous hedge maze. Hale planted the first rows of yew when he was twenty-three, and over the following four decades the maze grew into something genuinely legendary: a half-mile tangle of pathways that has defeated grown adults and delighted thousands of children on school trips.

What makes Hale's story interesting is not the maze itself but his stubborn refusal to let anyone else manage it. He openly admits that he is poor at the business side of things, having never kept a proper set of accounts or hired a single employee in forty years, yet he has resisted every offer from the regional tourism board, insisting that the maze must be under his own control for as long as he is able to prune it himself.

The problem is that Hale is now seventy-eight, and almost nobody anticipates that he will be trimming hedges himself for another decade. In an effort to prepare for that eventuality without stripping away what makes the maze special, the town council recently proposed an unusual compromise: a paid apprentice would train directly under Hale for five years, learning the exact pruning patterns by hand, with no cameras, no spreadsheets, and no committee meetings involved until Hale himself decided the apprentice was ready.

Supporters of the plan argue that this is the only realistic way to keep the maze's particular character alive once Hale can no longer maintain it. Critics counter that formalizing any part of the process, even an apprenticeship, will eventually invite exactly the kind of standardized management Hale spent his life avoiding, and that a maze trained into someone else's hands can never truly be the same place people recollect from their childhoods.

Both sides may be right, and that tension is probably unavoidable. Whatever happens to Hale's maze, the lesson for anyone running something similarly personal is this: the moment you stop planning for who comes after you, you have quietly decided the thing you built dies with you, however legendary it was while it lasted.
