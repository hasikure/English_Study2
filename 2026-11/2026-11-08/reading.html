English Text

When Coach Reyes took over our community volleyball program, the first thing he did was draw a pentagon on the whiteboard and explain the five-player rotation he wanted us to run for the entire season. It looked elegant, and within two months, it clearly worked: we won more matches in a single autumn than the club had won in the previous three years combined, and the trophy case in the lobby started filling up with new silverware faster than anyone could polish it.

What took longer to notice was how persistent Coach Reyes' training actually was, in the least healthy sense of the word. Practices ran long, players who said they felt unwell were quietly pushed to finish anyway, and injuries that should have ended someone's week on the bench were treated as minor inconveniences to work through. The breaking point came when one of our strongest players collapsed from dehydration after a practice in the summer heat and had to be taken to the hospital for intravenous fluids before anyone fully understood what had happened.

Several parents, understandably, called the coaching staff's approach irresponsible, and I think they were right. A coaching philosophy is not actually working if it produces silverware at the cost of a player ending up in a hospital bed. Persistence is supposed to describe effort applied wisely over time, not a refusal to notice when a body is clearly asking for rest.

The club eventually brought in a sports medicine consultant, rewrote the training schedule, cutting practice length and adding mandatory recovery days, and began selling modest team merchandise in the lobby to help fund the new equipment. Surprisingly, the team kept winning, sometimes by even wider margins than before. The pentagon rotation Coach Reyes drew on that first whiteboard turned out to be genuinely effective, which was never really the question. The real question was whether winning justified running players into the ground to get there, and the answer, once we actually asked it, was obviously no.

Looking at that trophy case now, I notice something I never did before: every piece of silverware in it represents both a win and, for at least one season, a program that mistook stubbornness for persistence and got away with calling it coaching.

何人かの保護者が、当然ながら、コーチ陣のやり方を無責任だと非難し、私もその通りだと思います。コーチングの哲学は、選手を病院のベッドに追い込む代償でトロフィーを生み出しているのなら、実際には機能していないのです。ねばり強さとは本来、時間をかけて賢く注がれる努力を指すはずのもので、体が明らかに休息を求めているのに気づかないふりをすることではありません。
