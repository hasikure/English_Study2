Last spring, a few hundred professional translators gathered in a luxurious conference hotel outside Geneva for what the organizers optimistically called "The Future of the Profession" symposium. The tone in the opening session was tense, because for the past two years, automatic translation tools have moved from occasionally useful to good enough that clients increasingly see a human translator as an unnecessary expense.

One attendee, a literary translator named Renata, described how a publishing house once came close to having her prosecuted for breach of contract, after an editor mistakenly believed she had used an unauthorized machine-translation tool to produce a manuscript faster than any human reasonably could. In due time the misunderstanding was cleared up: Renata had simply worked eighteen-hour days for three weeks, which, as she put it dryly, is a far less glamorous explanation than secretly outsourcing the job to software.

The symposium's real argument was not about any single incident but about which direction the profession should move in next. Some speakers insisted that translators should go to extremes in resisting the tools altogether, refusing to touch machine output under any circumstances, on the theory that any cooperation legitimizes their own eventual replacement. Others argued for the opposite extreme: full, immediate adoption, treating human review as a temporary and shrinking role.

Renata's own position, which drew the loudest applause of the two days, rejected both extremes. She described how her own workflow has altered little by little over eighteen months: she now runs a first machine pass on technical passages where precision matters more than voice, then rewrites dialogue and tone entirely by hand, because that is where her actual expertise still clearly outperforms any tool. The adjustment was not dramatic or sudden, she said, but a long series of small decisions about which sentences to trust to software and which to protect.

What struck me most, listening from the back of that luxurious ballroom, was how unglamorous the real debate turned out to be. Nobody at the symposium had a tidy answer to whether translation as a profession would still exist in ten years. What they had instead was Renata's quieter claim: that the translators who survive this transition will not be the ones who picked a side and defended it loudly, but the ones willing to keep altering their habits, little by little, for as long as it takes to find out.
