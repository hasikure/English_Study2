English Text

My cousin Elena decided to wed last June, and from the moment she announced her plans, most of our relatives assumed the ceremony would take place at some posh countryside hotel with crystal chandeliers and a string quartet. That assumption made sense, since nearly every wedding our family had attended in the past decade had followed exactly that script. It is all too easy to believe that a memorable wedding requires an expensive, polished venue, but Elena's choice proved that assumption wrong, and the reasons behind it taught me something about what actually makes a ceremony feel genuine.

Instead of a hotel ballroom, Elena and her partner married at a small berry farm two hours outside the city, the very farm where her grandfather had grown strawberries and raspberries for forty years. The altar itself was nothing more than two wooden posts wrapped in ivy, standing at the edge of a field still heavy with ripening berries, and the guests sat on hay bales instead of gilded chairs. When I first heard the plan, I privately labeled it a downgrade, picturing mosquitoes and midday heat instead of air conditioning and canapés. I was wrong to assume that.

By the time the ceremony began, most of us had rolled up our sleeves to help set up tables among the rows, arms already showing a faint tan from the afternoon sun, and nobody seemed to miss the chandeliers at all. The first reason the farm worked so well is that it carried real meaning: every guest understood exactly why the couple had chosen it, rather than simply admiring an impressive building they had no connection to. The second reason is that simplicity lowered the pressure on Elena and her partner; with no elaborate seating chart or strict schedule to manage, they spent the afternoon actually talking with their guests instead of rushing between photographs. The third reason is that doing physical work together, carrying chairs, picking berries for the dessert table, made the day feel shared rather than merely watched.

None of this means expensive venues are wrong for everyone. But it does suggest that we are all too quick to label a wedding "real" only when it looks a certain way, and too slow to notice that meaning, not polish, is usually what people remember years later.
