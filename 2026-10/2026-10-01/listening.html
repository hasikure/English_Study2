When I retired last spring, I decided, in accordance with a promise I had made to myself years earlier, that I would spend my mornings in the garage instead of in front of the television. My wife had always said I looked more youthful whenever I had a half-finished project on the workbench, and I think she was right; keeping my hands busy felt far preferable to simply resting all day. The first thing I did was open an old wooden chest that I had been interested in for months but never had time to touch. Inside were my father's carpentry tools, rusty but still usable, the same ones he had hung on to for over thirty years before passing them down to me. At first I only meant to clean them, but within a week I was repairing an old chair, then a small table, then a bookshelf that had been wobbling in the hallway since I was a teenager. None of these jobs paid me anything, and none of them needed to. What mattered was that each evening I went to bed tired in a good way, the kind of tiredness that comes from making something rather than from simply getting older. My children sometimes ask why I do not just relax now that I have the time, and I tell them that for me, staying busy with my hands is exactly what relaxing looks like.
