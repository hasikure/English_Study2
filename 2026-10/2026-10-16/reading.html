English Text

At first, when I was called to testify in a small local case about illegal logging near my hometown, I assumed my testimony would barely matter. I had only seen a few trucks a couple of times, and the whole thing felt like someone else's problem, the kind of elemental force of nature, like weather or erosion, that simply happens to a landscape over time rather than something any one person is directly responsible for. That assumption did not survive the morning I actually sat in the witness box, under oath, and was asked to describe, in specific detail, what I had watched happen to the hillside behind my parents' farm.

The lawyer representing the local conservation group was not interested in my general impressions. She wanted dates, truck counts, the exact slope where the tree line used to supply shade for a stream that had since dried into mud. Answering her questions honestly made me realize how thoughtless I had been about something happening practically in my own backyard: I had noticed the deforestation, mentioned it occasionally at dinner, and then simply moved on, the way people do with problems that feel too large and too slow to actually act on.

The council chamber was in session for most of that day, and by the afternoon, several other neighbors had testified to the same pattern: trucks arriving at dawn, permits that never seemed to match the number of trees actually cut, and a stream that had quietly stopped running sometime the previous winter. None of us, individually, had enough evidence to prove anything. Together, under oath, our small observations started to supply exactly the kind of pattern the court needed.

The company's logging permit was eventually suspended, though the hillside itself will take decades to recover. What stayed with me afterward was not the verdict, but how easily I had let myself treat an obviously man-made problem as though it were simply elemental, inevitable, something that happened to the land rather than something any of us chose to look away from.
