English Text

When my cousin, a wildlife biologist, first told me she had spent a summer counting eagle carcasses near a newly built wind farm, I assumed she was building a case against offshore wind power entirely. She was not. The facility in question, she explained, was not actually offshore at all, but sat directly across a narrow mountain pass that local eagles had used as a migration route for longer than anyone had kept records. Contemporary turbine design, she argued, is not inherently dangerous to birds; it is specific sites, chosen for easy construction access rather than careful study of wildlife patterns, that turn ordinary turbines into unacceptable hazards.

She had an itch to prove this with numbers rather than opinion, so she spent three years comparing eagle mortality at a dozen wind farms across the region. The pattern she found was almost embarrassingly simple: the sites genuinely offshore, built miles out at sea where few birds ever travel, killed almost no eagles at all. The sites built along mountain passes and ridgelines, chosen because they were cheaper to supply with construction equipment, were responsible for nearly every documented death. In other words, communities were not being burdened with an impossible choice between clean energy and wildlife protection; they were being burdened with the consequences of lazy site selection.

What frustrated her most was how little it would have cost to avoid the problem. Not one additional ounce of steel or a single extra turbine was needed, just a willingness, before construction began, to study where eagles actually flew. Instead, several of the worst-sited projects were approved quickly by officials eager to meet contemporary renewable energy targets, treating a wildlife survey as an unnecessary delay rather than a basic precaution.

Her conclusion was not that offshore wind power should be abandoned, but that it should be judged by its best examples rather than its worst. An industry serious about its own future, she told me, cannot afford to treat even a single avoidable eagle death as acceptable collateral damage.
