When I visited my grandfather in the hospital last week, the first thing he told me, half joking, was that the only good thing about being stuck in bed all day was finally having time to beat everyone at chess. He has always excelled at the game, and even propped up against three pillows, he still managed to beat the nurse twice before lunch. I asked him whether the staff ever got tired of being at the beck and call of patients who ring the bell every ten minutes, and he laughed and said the nurses on this ward never seemed to stop smiling, no matter how busy they got. While we talked, a tea trolley came rattling down the corridor, and my grandfather lit up immediately, the way he always does around biscuits. He told me the pain in his shoulder had finally started to cease after a new round of physiotherapy, and that he expected to be home within the week. Before I left, he asked me to give his best regards to my mother, who had not been able to visit that day because of work, and he made me promise to bring the chess board next time so he could, in his words, teach me a proper lesson. Walking out of the hospital, I found myself oddly reassured: whatever else had changed, his competitive streak clearly had not.
