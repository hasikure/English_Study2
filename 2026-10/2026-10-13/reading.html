English Text

Working backstage at a small theatre company last year taught me more about clothing than a decade of shopping ever had. Actors would arrive undressed from their street clothes and emerge minutes later in costumes that had to survive eight shows a week, which meant every seam had to be built for abuse. The costume supervisor, a woman named Odalys who had sewed professionally for thirty years, was the one who first explained to me what tailors mean by "the hang" of a garment: not just how it looks on a hanger, but how the fabric falls and moves once a real body is inside it.

Odalys had little patience for fast fashion, and she made her case in a way that stuck with me. Cheap clothing, she argued, exists because our fascination with constantly buying something new happens to be an easy habit to exploit, and the environmental cost is a genuine drain on water, cotton fields, and factory labor, all so a shirt can be worn five times before falling apart. Whether a brand was expensive or cheap was, to her, largely irrelevant to the real question: had anyone actually built the garment to endure?

She showed me two shirts side by side, one mass produced and one she had made herself from a pattern she had drafted thirty years earlier. The hand-stitched one still hung perfectly on its hanger, its seams unmoved despite hundreds of wears, while the cheaper one had already lost its shape after a handful of washes. The difference in quality, she said, was roughly in proportion to the hours someone had spent actually thinking about how the garment would behave on a moving body, not sitting still in a shop window.

I will not pretend I have given up buying cheap clothes entirely since that conversation. But I can no longer look at a five-dollar shirt without wondering what corners were cut to get it that low, and I have started asking, before buying anything, whether I am paying for something enduring or simply paying to feel briefly fascinated by something new.

オダリスはファストファッションに対してほとんど我慢がきかず、彼女の主張は私の心に強く残りました。安価な衣服が存在するのは、常に新しいものを買い続けたいという私たちの魅惑が利用しやすい習慣だからにすぎず、環境面のコストは、水や綿花畑、工場労働に対する本当の消耗であり、それもこれも、シャツが5回着られただけでボロボロになってしまうためだ、と彼女は主張しました。ブランドが高いか安いかは、彼女にとっては、本当に重要な問いとはほとんど無関係でした。その問いとは、誰かが実際にその衣服を長持ちするように作ったのか、ということでした。

Notes

  • "the hang of" a garment is a tailoring term for how fabric drapes and moves on a body; the same phrase, "get the hang of something," is also a common idiom meaning to learn how to do something.
  • "drain" here is used as a noun meaning something that uses up or depletes a resource (e.g. "a drain on water"), not the physical pipe that carries water away.
