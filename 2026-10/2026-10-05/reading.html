English Text

When people learn that I can play the violin reasonably well, the first question they usually ask is whether I come from a musical family, as if talent must be inborn rather than built. I understand why they ask: my teacher, a fellow countryman of the composer Dvořák who had moved to our town decades earlier, often seemed to produce sound effortlessly, and it was tempting to assume his gift was simply biological, something hard-wired into his hands before he ever picked up a bow. But the more time I spent in his studio, the more I realized that his ease came from thousands of hours of unglamorous repetition, not from any special inheritance.

I remember one lesson in particular when I complained that a passage felt impossible, and he reproved me for giving up before I had even tried ten different fingerings. Students must submit to that kind of blunt correction if they want to improve, he explained; comfort and progress are rarely related to each other. He also pointed out that when strangers learned he was not related to any famous musician whatsoever, they were always surprised, since they had assumed his skill had to run in the family. In fact, he told me, nobody in his family had ever previously played an instrument, and he himself had been considered the least promising student in his first class.

What struck me most was his claim that no musician is ever truly free of effort, no matter how early they started or how encouraging their environment was. The students who improved fastest, in his experience, were not the ones with the most obvious inborn ease, but the ones willing to submit to correction, practice the same four bars for an hour, and keep returning the next day regardless of how discouraging the previous lesson had felt.

I still cannot say for certain how much of skill is biological and how much is built through sheer repetition. But I no longer assume, as I once did, that someone's ability is simply inborn. More often than not, what looks like a natural gift is really just years of patient, occasionally painful practice that nobody saw.

ある時のレッスンで、あるフレーズがどうしても弾けないと私が訴えたとき、まだ10種類も指使いを試していないのに諦めかけているといって、先生に叱られたことを覚えています。上達したいなら、そうした遠慮のない指摘に従わなければならない、と先生は説明してくれました。快適さと上達は、めったに関係し合うものではないのだと。また先生は、見知らぬ人たちが「彼が有名な音楽家と縁もゆかりもない」と知ると、いつも驚かれると話してくれました。周囲は、彼の技術は家系によるものに違いないと思い込んでいたからです。実際には、彼の家族でそれ以前に楽器を演奏した人は誰もおらず、彼自身、最初のクラスでは最も見込みのない生徒だと見なされていたそうです。

私が最も心を打たれたのは、どんなに早く始めていようと、どんなに恵まれた環境にいようと、努力を免れている音楽家など一人もいないという彼の主張でした。彼の経験では、最も早く上達する生徒は、最も明らかな生まれつきの器用さを持つ生徒ではなく、進んで指摘を受け入れ、同じ4小節を1時間練習し、前回のレッスンがどれほど落ち込むものであっても、また翌日に戻ってくる生徒だったのです。

技術のうちどれだけが生物学的なもので、どれだけが純粋な反復によって築かれるものなのか、私には今も確信が持てません。けれども、かつての私のように、誰かの能力を単に生まれつきのものだと決めつけることはもうありません。多くの場合、生まれつきの才能のように見えるものは、実は誰にも見られていない、何年にもわたる忍耐強く、時に苦しい練習にすぎないのです。
