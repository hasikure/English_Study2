English Text

My nephew Daniel used to be, by his own admission, something of a cyberaddict: before he turned fourteen, he was checking his phone on a regular basis, sometimes more than once a minute, and nobody in our family seemed particularly alarmed by it. We assumed, the way most adults do, that it was simply what teenagers did now, and that getting upset about it would accomplish nothing. I used to be indifferent to it myself, until the week Daniel's school called his parents in for a meeting.

The teacher's concern was not really about screen time in the abstract; it was about how little Daniel managed to do outside of his phone. He had stopped meeting friends in person, stopped finishing homework on time, and seemed anxious whenever his battery dropped below twenty percent. The school counselor made an argument that stuck with me: apps are not accidentally addictive, she said; they are built by people who understand, in painstaking detail, exactly which notifications and colors are hard-wired to grab human attention, and teenagers are simply easier targets than adults.

What changed things for Daniel's family was less dramatic than I expected. Rather than confiscating the phone outright, his parents set a single, clear rule: only one screen at a time, and never during meals. Daniel also became eligible for a local youth program that met twice a week specifically to build offline hobbies, from woodworking to basketball, and surprisingly, he chose to join on his own once he realized nobody there was staring at a phone either.

None of this solved everything overnight. Daniel still checks his phone constantly, and probably always will to some degree. But he now manages to put it away during dinner without being reminded, and his parents say the arguments about screen time have mostly disappeared. Watching this unfold, I no longer think indifference is a reasonable response from any of us, parents, teachers, or distant uncles. The habits being built into a generation of phones are not neutral, and pretending otherwise just leaves families to solve the problem alone, one exhausted argument at a time.
